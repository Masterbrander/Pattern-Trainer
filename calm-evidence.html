What this feature is, and is not

These are original, short, plain-language self-help scripts spoken by your device. They are not recordings from a therapist, licensed treatment protocols, diagnoses, or a substitute for treatment. The exact scripts, pacing, Random function, and app have not been clinically tested. Evidence for an ingredient does not establish that a new three-minute combination has the same effect. There is no guarantee that any exercise will reduce a particular episode.

The practical target is a little more choice before acting, not compulsory relaxation or immediate happiness. The scripts are written for adult self-use. They have not been validated specifically for ADHD or for children. Review suitability and wording with your therapist before treating them as part of a care plan or sharing them for clinical use.

Why these four categories

Bring me down: strongest fit to the anger target

Kjærvik and Bushman's 2024 meta-analysis included 154 studies and 10,189 participants. Arousal-decreasing activities reduced anger/aggression on average, whereas arousal-increasing activities were ineffective overall. This supports an approach that reduces tension rather than rehearsing grievances more intensely. It does not establish the effectiveness of these specific scripts. [1]

Fincham et al. pooled 12 randomized trials involving 785 adults for the stress outcome and found a small-to-medium average benefit of breathwork. Most studies had moderate risk of bias. The interventions, populations, and doses varied. [2] Balban et al. studied five minutes of daily breathwork over a month; that is not a test of a one-off anger episode. These scripts do not reproduce its cyclic-sighing protocol or claim the same results. [3]

Implementation: easy breathing, optional slightly longer exhalation, and releasing unnecessary muscle tension. One variant uses no breathing counts. No forced deep breaths, breath holds, rapid breathing, or cold-water challenges. NHS guidance emphasizes gentle, comfortable breathing. [4] A small trial of 20-minute relaxation recordings supports relaxation as a component, not the efficacy of our shorter release-only adaptation. [5]

Ground me: clinically recommended, with a narrower direct evidence claim

WHO's stress-management guide is evidence-informed and field-tested, and includes grounding and unhooking skills. [6] NHS inform offers sensory grounding for overwhelming anxiety and stress. [7] These are useful guidance sources, not proof that a specific sensory checklist reliably stops anger. Our scripts use contact, sound, visual orientation, and present surroundings without requiring a rigid checklist. They are adaptations, not a validated 5-4-3-2-1 protocol.

Implementation: notice feet, support, texture, nearby sounds, or neutral objects. Eyes remain open for visual orientation. Other guides allow open or closed eyes where safe. If body-focused attention feels worse, the script offers external attention instead.

Get me unstuck: experimental support for changing perspective

Two laboratory experiments by Mischkowski, Kross and Bushman found that a self-distanced perspective after provocation reduced angry/aggressive responses compared with self-immersion or controls. [8] This is relevant to angry thought loops, but the lab tasks are not the user's everyday life or these exact scripts. WHO's unhooking framework supplies an additional practical basis. [6]

Implementation: name the repeating thought briefly, distinguish noticing a thought from obeying it, take a neutral-observer or caring-adviser perspective, and select one controllable next action. The script does not decide whether the user is right, blame the user, demand forgiveness, or encourage extended replay of the provoking event.

Make me laugh: an optional experiment, not an equally established treatment

Samson and Gross found that good-natured humor regulated responses to negative pictures more effectively than mean-spirited humor in two experiments. [9] That is indirect evidence for an acute anger episode. It does not prove that humorous audio, dark humor, or a particular joke will work. A spontaneous laugh that helped once is useful personal information, not a clinical validation study.

Implementation: harmless absurd scenarios involving a committee, announcer, documentary, or help desk. No targeting real people, shame, insults, or compulsory positivity. The scripts explicitly allow switching away when humor feels dismissive or aggravating. Random includes this category because the user requested it, not because the evidence is equal across all categories.

Rant capture is separate from regulation

The anger meta-analysis does not support cathartic venting as an anger-management remedy. [1] Rant remains a way to capture words for later reflection. Calm does not claim that longer, louder, or more intense venting is therapeutic. If recording is increasing activation, stopping and taking space is an available choice.

Safety and usability choices

Use only while stationary and away from driving, machinery, or other situations requiring full attention. No guide requires closing the eyes. Breathing is optional and comfortable; stop pacing and return to normal breathing if dizzy, breathless, or more distressed. Move away from immediate conflict when safe. If anyone is in immediate danger or the user cannot stay safe, the app is not the appropriate level of help.

Keep the app on screen. It asks for a screen wake lock where supported, but this is best-effort. Leaving the app pauses the guide. Pause cancels the current utterance; Resume repeats the current instruction rather than relying on inconsistent native pause handling. Stop cancels speech and pending pauses. Read instead makes progression manual. Audio failures show an error, not a false completion message.

The scripts are fixed and reviewable. Calm does not send the rant, private entries, or ratings to an LLM to generate speech. Voice synthesis uses the device/browser voice selected. Some voices need a network connection. Prefer a downloaded local English voice and test it in airplane mode; offline spoken output is not guaranteed.

What is saved and what the numbers mean

A separate Calm record stores the guide/version ID, choice or Random, timestamps, completion status, optional before/after ratings and baseline source, and optional feedback. It does not create another anger incident. A started guide may be unfinished, switched, completed, or ended early. Stopping early is not automatically counted as success or failure.

Random starts without requiring a rating. A rating recalled afterward is labeled **recalled**. The displayed before-to-after average uses only valid ratings taken before and after completed or early-ended sessions. Unrated, recalled, switched, and interrupted pairs are excluded. The table is descriptive, not a ranking of treatments. A drop could reflect time passing, context, expectation, another action, or selection bias. No weighted-random personalization or AI training is implied.

Calm records are stored separately on the device, scoped to the signed-in account, queued for sync, and included in V7 backup exports. A new owner-protected Supabase table holds the cloud copy. Guest use plays the guides but does not retain a personal Calm record. Removing a Calm history row hides it through a synced deletion marker; it is not a promise of immediate removal from provider backups. Cloud sync is not a versioned disaster-recovery backup.

All 16 original scripts

Pauses below follow each spoken instruction. Actual duration varies with the selected voice. These are the exact words used in the app.

Feet, seat, sound (ground)

Sources: 6, 7.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Sit or stand somewhere steady. Keep your eyes open if that feels better. Closing them is optional, never a requirement. Pause 4s.
  3. Find where your feet contact the floor. Notice pressure at your heels and at the front of each foot. You are looking for ordinary sensations, not a special feeling. Pause 10s.
  4. Now notice the support under you: a chair, the floor, or the back of a seat. Let that support take some of your weight. Pause 8s.
  5. Rest one hand on the other, or on a stable surface. Notice texture and temperature. Do not grip harder; just notice contact. Pause 10s.
  6. Pick out one sound nearby. Then another farther away. No need to decide whether you like them. Let each sound be just a sound. Pause 12s.
  7. The argument may come back into your attention. That is not a failed exercise. Name it: there is the argument again. Return to the pressure under your feet. Pause 10s.
  8. Notice your hands once more. Then your feet. Let your breathing happen normally. You do not need to change or monitor it. Pause 10s.
  9. Choose one safe, ordinary action for the next minute. Stay seated, put an object down, or ask for a break. Keep the decision small. Pause 8s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
Read the room, not the argument (ground)

Sources: 6, 7.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Keep your eyes open for this guide. Look around the place you are actually in. If looking around is uncomfortable, use the sounds and contact points instead. Pause 4s.
  3. Find three objects that have straight edges. Describe their shapes silently. Leave the story about the other person out of it for a moment. Pause 12s.
  4. Find two different colors. Notice where the color changes at the edge of each object. You do not have to search the whole room. Pause 10s.
  5. Notice one surface touching your body. It could be your shoe, sleeve, chair, or the floor. Describe that contact in plain physical terms. Pause 10s.
  6. Find the quietest sound you can hear. Then a louder one. Hearing either of them is enough; there is no correct answer. Pause 10s.
  7. Say to yourself where you are, and what you are doing right now. Not what happened earlier. For example: I am standing in this room, taking a short break. Pause 8s.
  8. Your attention may jump back to the problem. Bring it back to one edge, one color, and the support beneath you. Pause 10s.
  9. You can return to the issue without answering it immediately. For now, choose what will keep the next minute from becoming harder. Pause 8s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
Something solid (ground)

Sources: 6, 7.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Put down anything sharp, breakable, or distracting. Rest your hands on your legs or on a stable surface. Eyes open or closed, whichever is more comfortable. Pause 5s.
  3. Notice your left hand. Where does it touch something? Is that surface smooth, rough, warm, or cool? Describe it without judging it. Pause 10s.
  4. Move your attention to your right hand. Notice the fingertips, the palm, and where the hand is supported. No need to change anything. Pause 10s.
  5. Feel both feet at the same time. Let your toes rest. You are checking what is physically here, rather than replaying what happened. Pause 10s.
  6. Listen to a sound that is already present. Follow it for a moment. If there is little to hear, return to the surface under your hands. Pause 12s.
  7. When a thought interrupts, acknowledge it once. Then choose contact or sound again. You are not required to clear your mind. Pause 10s.
  8. Let your jaw rest and make your grip less firm. Keep your breathing ordinary. Small changes are enough for this exercise. Pause 8s.
  9. Before you move on, choose a place to keep your attention during the next minute. Your feet, a neutral object, or a steady sound will do. Pause 8s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
Here, then next (ground)

Sources: 6, 7.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Stay somewhere steady. You can keep your eyes open throughout. Notice three places where your body is supported, such as feet, seat, and back. Pause 10s.
  3. Describe one of those contact points silently. Pressure, warmth, firmness, or texture. Stay with what you can actually notice. Pause 10s.
  4. Listen for one sound. Let it begin and end without explaining it. Then return to the support under you. Pause 10s.
  5. The aim is not to decide who was wrong. It is to bring some attention back to the present while the feeling is still here. Pause 6s.
  6. Name one thing happening now that is separate from the problem. An appliance is running. Light is coming through a window. Your hand is resting on your leg. Pause 10s.
  7. Notice your feet again. Let your hands open a little. If concentrating on your body feels worse, look at a neutral object instead. Pause 10s.
  8. What is one action available to you now? You can delay a reply, step into another room, or ask for time. Pick the smallest safe option. Pause 10s.
  9. Let the larger problem wait while you take that one action. A pause does not mean you approve of what happened. Pause 6s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
Less effort, slower out (down)

Sources: 1, 2, 4.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Sit comfortably. Eyes closed is optional. Keep each breath easy, not bigger. If this makes you dizzy, short of breath, or more distressed, stop the pacing and breathe normally. Pause 4s.
  3. Let your shoulders lower a little. Rest your hands. Leave a small space between your teeth rather than clamping your jaw. Pause 8s.
  4. Take an ordinary, gentle breath in. Let it out slightly more slowly, if that feels comfortable. No breath hold. No need to empty your lungs. Pause 10s.
  5. Do that again at your own pace. Easy in, unforced out. If a longer exhale feels wrong, use the same comfortable length in both directions. Pause 18s.
  6. Let the next few breaths be quiet and comfortable. This is not a test of lung capacity. Breathe again whenever you need to. Pause 20s.
  7. Check your hands and shoulders. Are you gripping or bracing? Let go of a little unnecessary effort while keeping your breathing easy. Pause 10s.
  8. Take a few more comfortable breaths. When the argument returns, come back to the next easy exhale rather than answering it. Pause 18s.
  9. Now stop adjusting your breathing. Let it settle into its own rhythm. Notice whether the tension has changed at all, without demanding that it disappear. Pause 8s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
Unclench first (down)

Sources: 1, 5.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. This one does not require breath counting. Breathe normally throughout. Sit or stand steadily, and keep your eyes open if you prefer. Pause 5s.
  3. Start with your hands. Open them gently and rest them on something. Do not stretch hard or work through pain. Just reduce the grip. Pause 12s.
  4. Notice your shoulders. Let them move away from your ears a little. There is no need to force them down. Pause 12s.
  5. Let your jaw rest. Leave your teeth slightly apart, if comfortable. Soften the muscles around your eyes and forehead rather than squeezing them. Pause 12s.
  6. Check your legs and feet. Release any unnecessary bracing while keeping yourself supported. You do not need to tense the muscles first. Pause 12s.
  7. Now return to your hands. You may have tightened them again. That is useful information, not something to criticize. Let them rest again. Pause 10s.
  8. Give the shoulders and jaw another chance to loosen. Leave your breathing alone. Stay with the smaller amount of effort for a few moments. Pause 18s.
  9. Notice what remains tense. Nothing has to be perfect. You can deal with the issue without keeping every muscle ready for it. Pause 8s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
A comfortable rhythm (down)

Sources: 1, 2, 4.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Keep your breaths small and comfortable. You can follow a count, but do not chase one. Stop counting and breathe normally if you feel dizzy, breathless, or more uncomfortable. Pause 4s.
  3. Try counting gently to three while breathing in, and to four while breathing out. No pause in between. Shorten either count whenever you need to. Pause 10s.
  4. Try another few breaths at that comfortable rhythm. The count is optional. Ease matters more than matching numbers. Pause 20s.
  5. Let your hands rest. Unclench your jaw. Keep the shoulders from doing extra work with each breath. Pause 10s.
  6. Use a few more easy breaths. If your attention wanders into the argument, notice that and return to the next breath without starting the argument again. Pause 20s.
  7. You can drop the count now. Let the out-breath be comfortable and unforced, even if it is no longer than the in-breath. Pause 15s.
  8. Notice the support underneath you. Let your breathing choose its own pace. Check whether you have a little less urge to react immediately. Pause 10s.
  9. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
A short physical reset (down)

Sources: 1, 5, 4.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Get your weight supported. Eyes closed is optional. Begin with ordinary breathing. You are reducing effort, not trying to breathe as deeply as possible. Pause 5s.
  3. Let your fingers uncurl. Rest them against your legs or a stable surface. Leave them there without squeezing. Pause 10s.
  4. Release a little tension in your jaw and forehead. Move your shoulders gently, then let them rest. Skip any movement that hurts. Pause 10s.
  5. On the next easy breath, let the exhale take its time. Do not hold the breath or push the air out. If breath focus feels worse, leave it alone and notice your feet instead. Pause 10s.
  6. For the next few breaths, keep the same small effort. You do not need to match my voice or any count. Pause 20s.
  7. Check whether your hands tightened again. Relax the grip and return to the support under you. That is the whole task for the next few moments. Pause 15s.
  8. Let breathing return to its usual rhythm. Notice the urge to act without acting on it yet. You can take more time before the next conversation. Pause 10s.
  9. Choose one safe next move that does not require winning or fixing everything. Putting down the phone for another minute is an option. Pause 8s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
Name it without obeying it (unstuck)

Sources: 6, 8.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Keep your eyes open or closed, as comfortable. You do not have to settle whether your thought is correct during this exercise. Pause 5s.
  3. Name the sentence that keeps coming back, once. Keep it short. Do not rehearse the full exchange or build a case against anyone. Pause 10s.
  4. Now put these words in front of it: I am noticing the thought that. Then name the thought once more. A thought can be important without being an instruction. Pause 10s.
  5. Notice one contact point, such as your feet on the floor. Give that sensation a few moments of attention while the thought is allowed to be there. Pause 12s.
  6. Imagine what you would advise someone you care about in this exact situation. Not who should win. What would help them avoid making things worse right now? Pause 12s.
  7. Pick one sentence from that advice. Keep it practical: wait before sending, ask one question, or take a break before speaking. Pause 10s.
  8. The thought may return before you feel settled. You can notice it again without supplying the next line of the argument. Pause 10s.
  9. Choose one action for the next few minutes. You can come back to the problem later, with more room to decide how to handle it. Pause 8s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
Take the outside view (unstuck)

Sources: 8, 6.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Leave your breathing alone. Feel the support beneath you. You can keep your eyes open. This is about perspective, not denying what happened. Pause 5s.
  3. Describe the situation as a neutral observer might. One sentence, using observable actions. Leave out guesses about motives and words such as always or never. Pause 12s.
  4. Now notice what this person needs in the next few minutes. More information? Some space? Time before responding? You are the person you are helping. Pause 12s.
  5. Ask what you would want this person to do so tomorrow is not harder. You do not have to excuse anyone else to answer that. Pause 12s.
  6. Look for one controllable action. Change where you are standing. Set the message aside. State that you need a break and will return to the conversation. Pause 10s.
  7. Notice whether your mind is trying to resume the argument. Return to describing the next action, rather than deciding the other person’s character. Pause 10s.
  8. Feel your feet or the chair again. Take a moment before deciding whether to act. No need to rush because a thought feels urgent. Pause 10s.
  9. Keep the next step small and safe. A different perspective is a tool to try, not a demand that you agree with anyone. Pause 6s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
Do not send it yet (unstuck)

Sources: 6, 8.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Set aside the message, tool, or conversation for a moment. Keep your eyes open or closed as comfortable, and let your hands rest. Pause 5s.
  3. Notice the reply you want to give. You do not need to say it aloud. Name the main urge once: correct them, defend yourself, leave, or force an answer. Pause 10s.
  4. An urge is something you notice. You can choose when and how to act on it. For this short break, no reply is required. Pause 10s.
  5. Ask what matters in this interaction after the heat has passed. Getting the task done? Being clear? Protecting the relationship? Keeping a boundary? Pause 12s.
  6. Choose one of those aims. Notice how the next action could serve it without trying to settle every part of the disagreement. Pause 12s.
  7. Now imagine advising a friend with the same aim. What short, useful action would you suggest? Give yourself that same option. Pause 12s.
  8. Feel the contact under your hands and feet. Let the reply wait a little longer. Not sending immediately leaves options open. Pause 10s.
  9. When you return, you can ask a question or state a boundary in a shorter sentence. You do not have to decide the exact wording now. Pause 6s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
One thing, not everything (unstuck)

Sources: 6, 8.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Leave the whole problem alone for a minute. Keep your body supported and your eyes however you prefer. The goal is a next step, not a verdict. Pause 5s.
  3. Name what happened in one brief sentence. Stop there. You do not need the entire history of the situation for this exercise. Pause 10s.
  4. Notice the meaning your mind added. For example: I am being ignored, I have failed, or this will never change. Name it as a thought, not a confirmed conclusion. Pause 12s.
  5. Ask which part you actually control during the next five minutes. Your next words, whether you send the message, where you stand, or whether you take more time. Pause 12s.
  6. Pick one action from that small list. Make it specific enough that you could do it without solving the bigger issue. Pause 12s.
  7. Imagine someone you care about taking that step. Would you insist they fix everything first, or let the small useful step count? Pause 10s.
  8. Let the original thought exist without answering it again. Notice your feet and your hands while giving yourself another short pause. Pause 12s.
  9. Return to the step you chose. You can decide later what else needs attention. You have not given up the problem by pausing it. Pause 6s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
The emergency committee (humor)

Sources: 9, 8.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. Try a little absurdity, not a joke at your expense. If humor feels irritating or the situation is serious, skip it and choose a grounding guide. Pause 4s.
  3. Picture your sense of urgency as a tiny emergency committee. They have called an all-hands meeting. Nobody brought an agenda, but someone has ordered three more whiteboards. Pause 7s.
  4. The committee has a proposed action plan: repeat the same argument, at greater volume, until the laws of reality give in. Excellent use of office supplies. Questionable project management. Pause 8s.
  5. No need to fire the committee. Tell them the decision is paused while the person actually responsible takes a short break. That person is you. Pause 8s.
  6. Give the committee one ridiculous administrative task. Alphabetize the paperclips. Prepare a forty-page report on why the stapler sounds judgmental. Let it be harmless and silly. Pause 12s.
  7. Notice whether there was even a small shift in your attention. A laugh is optional. No change is also an honest result. Pause 10s.
  8. Let the imaginary meeting fade. Feel your feet or the seat. Loosen your hands. Whatever happened still matters, but the meeting does not get to choose your behavior. Pause 10s.
  9. Choose one real action that needs less drama than the committee proposed. Waiting before replying is a perfectly workable action. Pause 8s.
  10. Check whether you have a little more room to choose your next action. You do not have to feel good. If you are still too wound up, keep the break going. You can stop here or try a different guide. Pause 3s.
The unnecessary sports commentary (humor)

Sources: 9, 7.

  1. Only use this while stopped, away from driving or machinery. If anyone is in immediate danger, get help first. For now, you do not need to solve the problem. Give yourself a little time before acting. Pause 2s.
  2. This is a short experiment in harmless absurdity. Do not aim the joke at yourself or another person. You can switch guides immediately if this is not the moment. Pause 4s.
  3. Imagine an overenthusiastic sports announcer describing a completely ordinary object nearby. A chair. A cup. The edge of a desk. The stakes are impressively nonexistent. Pause 8s.
  4. The cup remains on the table. Incredible composure. The chair has made no attempt to escalate. Commentators are calling it a masterclass in sitting there. Pause 8s.
  5. Let the announcer describe your next useful move: a hand opens, shoulders lower, a reply remains unsent. No penalties. The crowd is mostly furniture. Pause 10s.
  6. You do not need to laugh on command. The point is to let something other than the argument occupy the microphone for a few moments. Pause 12s.
  7. Now drop the commentary and notice the real object. Its shape, its position, and one simple detail you had not noticed before. Pause 12s.
  8. Notice whether the urge to react has changed at all. Let your breathing be normal and your hands rest without gripping. Pause 10s.
